# A Revenant

> English translation assisted by Claude AI

Are you awake? I'm your assigned guide. The tablet beside you holds information about this era — feel free to ask me anything.

Those things on your head... fox ears? Are they real?

They really are — watch, it moves with my will. But I used to be human, too. Later I converted into a demihuman.

Converted from a human? Isn't human experimentation banned? Which country's research produced demihumans like this?

No — generally speaking, the demihumans that exist now aren't the product of experimenting on people. They're made by modifying other species with technology that makes them more human-like. The range is huge, from thickly-furred "furries" to beastfolk who retain only a few traits. Biologically, they belong to neither the human race nor their original animal species — they're an independent species of their own. As for me, and others like me who were converted from humans, we came about by copying our memories at the time into a demihuman body.

Memory copying? Was it voluntary — for you?

Yes, of course. The current United Government places enormous weight on human free will. They forbid any form of coercion.

And the original person, after the copy is made? Doesn't it feel awful to see an exact copy of yourself?

...By statute, the original person has their memories stripped and is remolded, and the converted demihuman doesn't retain the pre-conversion memories either — they don't even know who they originally were. This is the United Government's "Memory Uniqueness" policy: no single memory may exist in duplicate at the same time.

Remolded? In what sense? It's not like being melted down and recast — that's for metal, not people.

Of course not — melting someone down would just make them a robot, and a person isn't a machine. Remolding means the memory is stripped, the body is destroyed, and within this Wall of Freedom, the memory is injected into a new human body.

Then what about me?

...The United Government is currently running the "Retrocession Project," reviving people from earlier eras relative to the present.

The Retrocession Project? You're trying to revive people from the past? Was I someone important in later history? Why pick me?

To make up for lost population, the United Government passed the Retrocession Project — using the dead to fill the numbers. Sorted from most recent death to earliest, about a hundred thousand ancients have been revived just today.

This "United Government" you keep mentioning — which country is it, exactly? One I know? Or some newly formed superpower?

Yes, and also not quite. It's a product formed by the merger of several nations you'd recognize, and it is also, currently, the sole highest governing system.

Merged into one? The nations I knew could never have gotten along well enough to merge. Did one side get wiped out by war? Or did it just collapse on its own?

Neither. After the Third World War, they realized they could never assimilate one another, and conflict from disagreement was unavoidable — so they used technology to build a wall. This "Wall of Freedom." People who disagree simply don't make contact, don't interact — as if the other side didn't exist in their world at all. The jointly-funded United Government is responsible only for maintaining peace and safeguarding human rights — that's the common denominator left among the surviving nations after the war.

Wall of Freedom? Another term you've thrown at me. This... is insane. Locking yourself in your own world — what kind of freedom is that? And if the United Government's only job is peace and human rights, why is it running the "Retrocession Project"? That doesn't add up!

They say they lost too much. They killed too many. They promised the population would never shrink again, only grow. And eventually their technology advanced enough to use observed historical slices to copy back the dead. I'm not sure whether a soul can really be regenerated. I hope so.

What are we — the revived — to them? You said we're just being used to fill the numbers, right? Are we just their performance metric? What happens to us?

Don't get worked up — I'm just a staff member. I used to think the same thing, but the United Government is just a machine that runs on its own now; it has little in the way of feeling, and it's not easily moved. As for you, there's no assignment. Like a newborn, you learn about this era and make your own choices — where to go, how to live. They don't concern themselves with that. And as your guide, all I can do is help you understand this era. No one gets to decide your path for you.

Make your own choices? So you chose to become a guide? Why? High barrier to entry? Good pay?

The pay isn't great. As for the barrier — you just need to be well-versed in this era and have some skills suited to the job. Like me: I studied and became fluent in 21st-century vocabulary, culture, and language. Being assigned to you wasn't a coincidence.

Hmm... how does it feel, being a demihuman? Since some people convert voluntarily, life must be better for them?

It isn't. Most people who become demihumans do it because they want to die — because as humans, they can't truly die. But ironically, once converted, they no longer have their old memories, so they forget why they wanted it in the first place. I'm probably an exception — before converting, I purchased human rights for my post-conversion self. That's why I can't die.

A person can't die? Not even voluntarily? What happened, that something this abnormal came to be?

The rights the United Government guarantees include the right to life, and on top of that, there's likely their promise that the population will never shrink again. When a body's functions stop out in the world, the everyone-has-one rights card senses it, sends the memory back, and injects it into an artificial body — identical to the original — inside the Wall. It's like a resurrection.

What kind of system is that? Can't they just throw the rights card away?

If the card gets too far from its holder, it moves itself back to their side.

So becoming a demihuman lets you escape it?

Not entirely. What they do is copy the memory from the instant of conversion onto the demihuman, then kill their original body — they believe that the memory from that exact instant carries their soul. Once you're a demihuman, you no longer hold human rights, and there's no mechanism to revive you. Of course, since the original body's functions have stopped, the memory from before is still sent back and injected into an artificial body inside the Wall. I don't know if that counts as release or not.

Is that what happened to you?

Probably not — otherwise it wouldn't make sense that "before converting, I purchased human rights for my post-conversion self." This, in fact, is my rights card.

You're certain this credit-card-looking silver thing moves on its own? What's the principle behind it?

It receives power wirelessly from the Wall and generates thrust. Basically, anywhere near a Wall of Freedom, it never runs short of energy.

That's remarkable. Now, about what you said earlier — demihumans don't have human rights? Aren't demihumans still human?

Not the same. Like I said, demihumans are the result of pre-war modification of other species to resemble humans. They can't produce offspring with humans, and biologically they don't count as human. Because of that, the United Government doesn't guarantee demihumans the full set of human rights. Fortunately, though, they're considered "half-human," so they do get the traditional right to survival, freedom, property, and freedom from slavery and torture.

That sounds close to the human rights of my own era. What's the difference from full rights?

As far as I know, there's a gap even from 21st-century rights. And now the gap is even bigger. The main thing is this: full rights let you enjoy abundant goods and resources without working — but it also means you can't die.

That's... is productivity even sufficient for that?

The United Government holds a huge and ever-growing number of intelligent robots, which provide massive productive capacity. According to official reports, even after subtracting all the resources spent on welfare, there's still a large surplus.

Isn't that just exploiting the robots? And if there's a surplus, why not distribute it to the demihumans?

I think it's exploitation too, but by their account: "Robots are built with 'serving humans' set as a desire; they act according to that desire, and since robots aren't people, there's no such thing as exploiting them." As for giving surplus to demihumans — the United Government machine is so massive and unwieldy it can't turn on a dime.

Calling "serving humans" a desire? You don't actually believe what the politicians say, do you? What's the reality?

That part is true, actually. If you get the chance, take a walk through the capital and you'll see it for yourself. The way they treat that service-urge — it's like how we treat sexual desire. Just watch how the robots react and you'll understand.

...So all the robots just indulge themselves freely out in public like that?

Not all of them. Just as some people choose celibacy, some intelligent robots do too. Most of the celibate ones end up in the Nation of No Desire — you can see them there.

The Nation of No Desire? Have you been? What's it like — a bunch of robots?

I've been. Besides robots, there are demihumans and humans there too — anyone pursuing asceticism tends to gather there. Though there are tourists as well. The citizens there say, "Whether you forbid others from coming, or don't forbid it, both are still desire." So they follow the same policy as the capital — no restriction on outsiders entering.

Were you pursuing celibacy yourself?

I went carrying a question, to seek an answer from a master — a tourist of sorts, I suppose. And it's not only the ascetics who settle there; wherever there's foot traffic, there's business, and the tourist services there are quite good.

What did you go to ask?

I was curious what the celibate robots thought about it. When I crossed the Wall of Freedom from another nation into the Nation of No Desire, the first thing I saw was a magnificent temple right at the center of the country, then a ring of smaller shrine buildings around it, and at the outermost ring, inns, restaurants, and markets.

A magnificent temple at the center? Aren't they supposed to be ascetics?

They didn't build the temple — merchants and seekers did. They said it felt more true to tradition than seeking enlightenment on barren land. So they pooled funds to build a scene matching the sutras' descriptions — "seven rows of railings, seven layers of netting, seven rows of trees." And the masters themselves don't mind either way — "whether pursuing wealth, or pursuing the absence of wealth, both are desire."

Hmm... so you went inside to ask about the Way too? How was it?

Clean, tidy, and there was a kind of solemnity to it. Everything was fine, except for the donation box they'd set up at the entrance — before you go in, they ask you to tap your rights card and pay them in the unified currency, calling it a "cleaning fee." I met the robot I was looking for in the main hall, and we had a deep conversation.

Don't they refuse to serve humans? Why would he agree to answer your questions?

"To refuse, and to not refuse — both are desire." That's what he'd say, in an even, level tone.

"Refuse" and "not refuse" — aren't those opposites? Logically, by the law of excluded middle, one of them must hold.

Logically, yes. But the point isn't refusing or not — the point is whether you act on your own initiative.

You let yourself get talked into mysticism? By your own logic, isn't "acting without initiative, on your own initiative" just... initiative? Can there even be a non-initiative kind of non-initiative?

You don't consciously track the state of every muscle while you walk — same idea. Or, when someone asks whether you've forgotten your ex: saying "I've forgotten!" means you still remember; asking "which one?" means you truly have forgotten.

Uh, just keep telling me what happened.

All right. One important part of traveling is lodging. I found a three-story wooden inn on a stone-paved road — the whole row of buildings there was wood-built. The exterior was wood, in look and feel and even temperature, but the material underneath was fireproof. The interior furnishings and décor were also wood in appearance, right down to the bed frame and the wardrobe. Even the service robots had a Chinese-human appearance, dressed like ancient Chinese maidservants. The whole place had a strong classical Chinese aesthetic — the only thing out of place was that you could control the furniture wirelessly with your rights card.

This rights card sounds a lot like a smartphone's functions.

The rights card really did evolve from the smartphone — it's just that now it's bound to every individual and issued uniformly by the United Government. It's absorbed almost every function: wallet, ID, communications, proof of ownership. Since the battery's been removed, it's powered wirelessly by the "Wall," light as a credit card. Look, here's its screen.

Since it's powered wirelessly by the Wall — couldn't you escape the rights card's constraints by fleeing somewhere without Wall signal?

Yes. But it's basically impossible — the Wall's signal covers essentially every region; you'd have to flee beyond the reach of space itself to escape it. And the rights card warns you before you leave signal range. Anyone who goes fully out of range is treated as missing and declared dead, and an identical new citizen is generated inside the Wall.

And those who go missing and then come back?

By regulation, if found, their memories from before the disappearance are erased, and they're treated as a new citizen with a new name.

...Are you hungry?

Hungry? You can order from the tablet beside you. And here — this is your rights card, just tap it to the tablet to authenticate.

Eat here on the bed? Is there a dining hall?

There is. You can eat in bed too, using the fold-out table attached to it, or at the visitor's table over there.

Let's go to the dining hall! Do you have anything else to attend to?

I'll take you to the dining hall. If you still have questions, I'm obligated to answer them.

I do still have plenty of questions, but don't you have other revived people to look after?

Guides are usually assigned as needed — once one case is finished, the next one begins.

In that case, isn't the ratio of guides to revived people pretty low? Basically one-to-one?

No. Typically, the revived ancients who are most common are the wary ones who won't speak to anyone, the ones desperate to find family and rush off, and the ones who quickly decide to go explore the world on their own. These need less of a guide's time, so there's no staffing shortage. And cases like yours, that need more time, exist too, but there are fewer of them. Most importantly, if there's ever a staffing gap, the robots are more than capable of taking on the role.

What about my parents?

I met them in the Nation of No Desire — they're there in retreat.

...Hmm, going by chronological order, shouldn't they have been revived after me?

...There are times when the Retrocession Project's ordering doesn't strictly follow sequence.

...Let's talk as we walk.

All right.

I hadn't noticed you were sitting the whole time. Are you walking on your fox tails now?

Isn't it only natural, when your tail is longer than your legs? Of course, I rotate among my ten tails.

Do all foxfolk do that?

Most foxfolk have shorter tails and walk on two legs — I'm a bit unusual.

I didn't notice until the door opened — the hallway and the room have different decor styles?

That's usually the case. Ancients and modern people think differently, and waking up to find yourself in a building completely beyond your own era can be a huge psychological shock. So the room's interior décor is matched to the architectural style of the era the person came from — like your 21st century: white-painted concrete walls, white ceramic flooring, paired with a movable hospital bed.

That man dressed in an Industrial-era gentleman's style — was he a revived person too?

That was a guide.

Why is his clothing different from yours?

Guides' outfits also shift according to the era of the ancient they're assigned to — that was 18th-century physician's attire. And I, as you can see, go with a white-coat style.

That view outside the window — is that a battlefield left over from the Third World War?

That's the United Government's capital. Though — yes, it is also a battlefield. Conflict remains unavoidable even now; the government just confines the fighting to within the capital. And the capital doesn't restrict the movement of people, goods, or information — it's a hub for a great deal of commerce, and home to plenty of people who enjoy war.

You all just calmly watch other people fight from the hallway? And doesn't the capital have administrative functions to carry out too?

The capital does carry administrative functions — the Wall surrounding this particular zone is especially thick and tall, and it houses most of the government functions. And the government watches the fighting partly to observe and record new weapons.

This is a lot like the Roman Colosseum.

It really is — the commercial district has live broadcasts, with matching betting available; you can place remote bets from here too.

How big is this place? I can't even see the edge.

The diameter is roughly a thousand kilometers — about the size of France in your era. Here we are — find a seat.

The design of your dining hall is strange — the columns are spaced and sized so irregularly.

Those are temporary private rooms. When no one's using them, it's open, like our table. You can also set this table to become its own private room — walls will materialize around it, blocking out light and sound.

No need. Looking at the menu the dining hall offers — the food really hasn't changed that much from my era!

The menu your rights card shows is adjusted per user, so what you're seeing is basically what was popular in the 21st century.

What's that? Besides the fox tails, you also have a flesh-colored... tentacle?

That's how I eat. It has a huge appetite — big enough to swallow a full-grown cow — and it supplies energy to my entire body.

So do you eat with your mouth at all?

The mouth is only for tasting; it has no digestive function. Whatever goes in through the mouth comes back out unchanged.

If I remember right, you're a remolded demihuman? Did you, as a human, choose this body yourself?

Barring some exception, yes — generally, when remolding happens, the demihuman body is chosen by the original person. Since remolding erases the prior memories, some people end up dissatisfied with the body they picked, but I'm quite happy with the one I have now.

I think I'd have plenty to talk about with the human you used to be, too.

You would.

You were telling me about staying at the wooden inn in the Nation of No Desire — then what?

After checking in, I went to the restaurant next door for dinner — it looked like it was run by the same owner — and ordered a bowl of plain rice porridge and a plate of pickled radish.

Doesn't the United Government provide free food? Why do you need to pay for porridge?

Scarce things still carry a price, to prevent demand from outstripping supply — eating at a tourist site itself costs money. It's only the dining halls inside the Wall that offer free food. Of course, there too you'd get the exact same plain porridge and pickled vegetables if you ordered them.

I can tell. Also — people passed out drunk in the dining hall — you just leave them be?

Sleeping in the dining hall is their own business; it's their freedom, and we don't interfere. Some people who don't like that sight simply block them out.

Personalized blocking? How does that work?

For humans, for instance — by interfering with the primary visual cortex, so they simply don't perceive them.

Mind control? Would that be done to me too?

No need to worry — those people agreed to the relevant terms. By default, no such interference is allowed.

After dinner, did you go out to look around?

No, I went back to the inn, wrote in my journal, and went to sleep. While chatting, the owner said, "A paper diary — you bought the skill for handwriting?" Even though almost no one writes by hand anymore these days, I still like keeping records on paper.

Your skills can be bought and sold? That's a bit cyberpunk — but aren't memories supposed to be one-of-a-kind?

Every skill carries its own unique memory, so it doesn't violate the uniqueness principle.

So people do sell their own memories? No wonder the owner was surprised — that must be quite expensive, right?

Most skills aren't expensive — they're generated by robots. A robot experiences something, and the resulting memory is extracted. In your era, didn't you use human-operated data to train robots? They don't serve dinner. After lunch I kept heading up toward the summit, and once there, went around the landing pad to find the masters inside the temple for a conversation. Sounds like a lot of trouble. With technology this advanced, isn't there a more convenient way? Like a video call to the master?

There used to be, but it's gone now. For most people who come here, seeking the Way is about the sense of participation and ritual — the content of the teaching itself matters less. That said, there are still people online who answer questions for free — a group of Mahayana Buddhists, and a group of New Jerusalem missionaries.

Gone? What went away?

Originally, merchants copied a robot master's memory and sold it online, but the returns weren't good. Later, they bought human rights for the master, moved him here, and built a commercial district around him — and only then did the profits pick up.

They can buy human rights for a robot?

As long as you have the money, you can buy human rights for pretty much anything. Mainly it's for demihumans, robots, and realized fictional characters given injected invented memories. For example, in your era, a virtual idol was also realized this way, and her fans crowdfunded to buy her human rights. There are also wealthy merchants who've bought rights for their dogs, even for the family car.

Wait, I'm a little confused. She was made real through technology, and then fans bought her human rights? What were they after?

Uniqueness. Once an individual has human rights, it can no longer be sold as a commodity, and no one can manufacture another realized idol like her — and the character herself is no longer under anyone else's control. Those merchants in the Nation of No Desire did the same thing — using uniqueness to eliminate the existence of copied master-memories online.

Sounds like a fight's broken out outside. Has the betting started?

Let me see... odds are two-to-one right now, not really worth betting on yet. Let's check again in a bit.

This seems different from sports betting. How do the odds move?

It's fairly interesting — the house first sets a prize pool on each side; this time it's a hundred thousand each. You pick a side to bet on, and the winners split the other side's pool proportionally, minus a percentage the house skims off. So let's wait a bit more — I think it might hit five-to-one.

Who's fighting this time? And why do you think it'll get to five?

The Fantasy Nation and the Sci-Fi Nation — both testing weapons this round. The two of them never really see eye to eye; they're always looking down on each other. One side calls the other "fools who can't understand," the other calls back "machines with no romance." I don't have any inside information for this one though, so I'm just betting cold.

Sounds fun, let me watch. You're waiting for the Fantasy Nation's odds to hit five before betting? Have you been to either of them?

I've been to both. Each has its own character, and they're both fairly advanced in their respective fields.

I'd like to hear about them sometime — what I'm more curious about is the Special Commercial Zone, sounds pretty thrilling. Would you recommend I go?

I can't decide that for you. That place isn't very friendly to people without money, so you'd want to settle in and save up before going.

How do you mean?

You can buy anything there, sell anything there — the whole atmosphere is... pretty cyberpunk.

Are there requirements to go there?

Not for tourists. But if you want to settle there, you need to hold at least one Bull Coin.

Bull Coin? Where does that funny name come from?

It's the currency the Special Commercial Zone issues — one coin equals the price of one set of human rights. So there's a whole subset of people doing leveraged Bull Coin trading. They wanted the market to always be "bullish," so they called it Bull Coin.

The price of human rights floats? Who sets it?

It used to be that applicants had to hand over their own assets — farmland, stocks, bonds, whatever — and let the United Government calculate how much those assets could earn. If the projected profit was enough to cover one person's welfare costs, rights were granted.

And now?

Once the Special Commercial Zone developed, that whole process got simplified. Now they just use the weighted interest rate the Zone's stock market publishes to calculate the price of rights, since their market is currently the largest — so the United Government uses it as the benchmark to anchor the overall price of rights.

So roughly how long would it take me to save up one coin?

At an ordinary income and savings rate, roughly two or three hundred years. So for most ordinary demihumans without rights, it's basically a pipe dream. But if you're interested, you can go as a tourist first — tourism there uses the standard currency, and the market is zoned separately too.

I'm curious — since Bull Coin is so expensive, what kinds of goods get priced in it?

Like I mentioned earlier — life experiences and memories produced by humans or demihumans. Seventy to a hundred years of them can sell for one Bull Coin; special ones go for even more.

So the rich pay to experience being poor? How ironic! And where does all the money the Special Commercial Zone collects go? Given their personality, I doubt they just let it sit and rot in a vault?

Of course not. According to them, it gets reinvested into the market to generate more returns, and then funds the welfare policies they themselves push for — taking care of the rightless demihumans. Because of that, people first jokingly called it "Sub-Coin," but that later clashed with the name of a currency issued by the Demihuman Nation, so they changed the name. The people who still call it that compete with each other over how many demihumans they personally support.

So they show off to each other over how much Bull Coin they have? What a scene! The Demihuman Nation — is that a country only demihumans can enter?

Not exactly. Its residents are mostly demihumans, but it's also open to non-demihuman tourists. If you go, just be careful — you can't have a record of anti-demihuman statements; they bar people like that from entering.

I recall demihumans aren't born with full human rights — did the United Government agree to let them found a nation? Or is it mostly made up of demihumans who do have full rights?

Not exactly either. Any group with enough votes can form its own nation. Votes come from only two sources: full human rights count as one vote, and standard currency equal in value to one Bull Coin counts as half a vote. The Demihuman Nation's vote base comes both from demihumans with full rights, and from concentrated wealth raised through issuing Sub-Coin.

Couldn't they use unclaimed wealth to buy full rights for their residents?

No — the population's too large; the ordering itself would become a new political problem. Deciding who gets it first is deciding who's "worth more."

I don't quite follow — if they pool votes centrally, does that mean the United Government operates as a form of representative democracy?

Something like that. Unlike your era, though, a national representative can split their own vote according to public opinion when making decisions — say, sixty percent in favor, forty against. And the votes a representative holds are themselves calculated from the nation's population and total assets.

In that case, wouldn't some nations restrict their own people from leaving, to consolidate power?

The United Government forbids that — everyone has the right to leave. Their assets, though, aren't guaranteed the same way. Nations that withhold assets don't get a good reputation abroad, so few people go to them — or people just move their assets out first, then go in themselves.

By that logic, are there almost no authoritarian states left?

No — people's yearning for belief and ideology is beyond what you'd expect. Those nations are highly assimilated, top to bottom.

I'd kind of like to see the Demihuman Nation myself — you've been, right? What's it like?

I have, though not to settle — just as a tourist. Let me think... when I stepped off the connecting airship...

Wait — an airship? You mean the kind from science fiction I know?

Something like that — also a means of moving through space. The Demihuman Nation occupies an entire planet, so taking an airship there isn't unusual.

Then... this capital we're in — is it on Earth too?

Don't worry, it's on Earth, yes. In the 21st century this spot was still ocean — right in the middle of the North Pacific.

Was there some massive tectonic event? Otherwise... even flattening the Tibetan Plateau wouldn't fill an area this large.

No, actually it would be enough — but that's not how it happened. There's now asteroid-mining technology; the soil here was brought back from space.

Why choose the North Pacific? A postwar decision? Or was it relocated at some point?

The nations that survived the Third World War were concentrated mainly around the Pacific Rim, and the Pacific is large enough to house a vast, independent capital zone. The location hasn't changed since the war.

The Third War... what happened? What about my hometown?

Your hometown... it wasn't a battlefield, but given the nature of the war, it was still damaged quite a bit. Basically only the advanced nations survived intact; the mid- and lower-tier nations largely became proxy battlegrounds, using weapons supplied by the advanced ones to fight each other.

Damage? Airstrikes?

No — airstrikes would basically mean open war, and everyone worked hard to keep the fighting from reaching their own civilians. So it was fought through information warfare — the goal wasn't territory, it was weakening the other side's willingness and ability to keep supplying weapons.

Information warfare — how does that damage a hometown? If the goal is weakening willingness, that should just mean anti-war propaganda.

That's part of it, but not all of it. Beyond turning a population against the war, it also targeted productivity directly — using social platforms and the entertainment industry to lower the enemy's birth rate and labor participation, undercutting their capacity to keep supplying weapons. There was also using social platforms to get a small portion of the enemy's own population to spontaneously commit sabotage — a method that, while highly destructive, wasn't used much.

That sounds like it would take a long time to take effect. And... it sounds like it might have already been happening back in the era I lived in?

They expected the Third War to be a long one, so they adopted this approach. As for your specific era, I'm not certain — some files remain unredacted classified, and it's unclear whether some things were natural phenomena or strategy. If you trace it to the earliest possible point, rumor has it it started around 2020.

And the places that were destroyed?

They turned into other kinds of nations, while the nations that survived intact were designated as Third War Preserves — they still operate as nations today, with their systems frozen at the state they were in when the war ended. Sharp eye. Once buildings get tall enough, waiting for a shared elevator takes too long, so now basically every building over 50 stories has switched to on-demand allocation. Like this — an empty elevator only gets dispatched here once someone is actually standing at the entrance waiting.
